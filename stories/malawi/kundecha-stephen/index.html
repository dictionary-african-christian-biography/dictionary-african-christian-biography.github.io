Kundecha, Stephen

c1876-c1945
Presbyterian
Malawi

Stephen Kundecha was a pioneering and scholarly Presbyterian minister who played a central role in the establishment and expansion of the church in the Zomba-Domasi area of eastern Malawi. A Yao by tribe, he grew up in the unsettled conditions of inter-tribal warfare during the 1870s and 1880s, witnessing the last major Ngoni raid into the Shire Highlands in 1884. Soon afterwards he was drawn to the newly established Domasi Mission where Alexander Hetherwick was setting up the first outpost of the Blantyre Mission of the Church of Scotland.

Hetherwick noticed that there was a small boy whose curiosity was bringing him to the new Mission each day to see what was going on. The two formed a connection and Hetherwick recalled the moment during the building of the mission station when Kundecha was watching him sharpening a plane iron. He used some oil from the sharpening stone to trace the sign of the cross on Kundecha’s forehead, telling him that he now had a new tribal mark and should start coming to school. The next morning Kundecha presented himself at the school and was enrolled as a pupil [1]. It was the first step on a journey that led to his baptism at Domasi in 1893 and his long service as a teacher, headmaster, minister and scholar.

In 1907 he was selected as one of only two men to be trained as the first African ministers of the Blantyre Mission. Along with his colleague Harry Kambwiri Matecheta, he enrolled on a four-year theological course. For the first two years their tutor was the Head of the Mission, Alexander Hetherwick. He made the theological class for the two men his top priority, complaining that it demanded three hours of teaching and two hours of preparatory work every day. The fact that he dedicated such time to this task amidst the many other demands that were made on the Head of the Mission, demonstrates how much he put a premium on the equipping of African leaders. When the two men wrote their first exams, Hetherwick remarked that, “It has far surpassed my utmost expectations, and confirms me in the policy that our Mission should adopt now – the extension of our sphere not by European stations but by native workers and native outstations” [2]. For their final two years from 1909 to 1911 they were taught by the brilliant young missionary Robert Napier [3]. On successful completion of his studies, Kundecha was ordained on 12 March 1911 at Zomba.

When the colonial government appointed a Commission of Inquiry to ascertain the causes of the Rising led by Baptist pastor John Chilembwe in 1915, Kundecha was summoned to appear for questioning [4]. In his testimony, he was clear about the differences between those like Matecheta and himself who opted to remain part of the European-run Missions and those like Chilembwe who chose to make a break and to operate independently of white control [5]. Points of contention included the understanding of baptism, appropriate dress for wives of educated Africans, the refusal of Chilembwe to join the Federated Board of Missions in Nyasaland, and the question of whether African ministers should work independently or under a European mission [6]. At the same time, Kundecha did not disown Chilembwe and made it clear that they had regarded each other as peers, even though they differed sharply on some points. At a time when the European community was eager to condemn Chilembwe as a scoundrel and renegade, Kundecha struck a note of mutual respect.

His own perspective on the colonial order was revealed in his account of a conversation he had with Chilembwe where the latter, “said that I, being myself a senior in the Mission for a long time, I ought to stand alone and work by myself, but I told him that we, being the scholars under the Europeans, we ought to be with them until they took the further step of going forward…” [7] Unlike Chilembwe, he was willing to operate under the prevailing order but not on an unconditional or indefinite basis. He looked forward to a “further step” when the time of being “under the Europeans” would come to an end. Despite clearly stated differences, this suggests that he had more in common with Chilembwe than he did with the colonial regime.

Much of his career was devoted to education. After being ordained as a minister, he was deployed by the Mission as a tutor for the next cohort of ministerial candidates, Joseph Kau¬nde, Thomas Maseya and Harry Mtuwa, who were ordained in 1916 [8]. He also drew on his Yao background to develop expertise in Islam. The missionary-scholar Thomas Price recognised Kundecha as, “… a genuine scholar who studied effectively to be a Christian minister, and made himself an authority on Islam who was respected by theologians from Zanzibar who came to Kawinga’s and Malemia’s for installation of chiefs of these names” [9]. His legacy can be found in Kundecha Village, named after him, where some of his descendants continue to live [10]; and in the flourishing Presbyterian churches found in the Zomba-Domasi area today.

Kenneth R. Ross
